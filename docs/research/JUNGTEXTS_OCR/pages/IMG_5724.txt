The capacity for unified description may extend beyond the physical
sciences to embrace all areas of human thought. As discussed in Part I, the
exploration of the archetypal hypothesis is currently underway in various areas
of the biological and social sciences. This development suggests the possibility
of a unification of these sciences with the physical sciences based on the ubi-
quity of the archetypes. However, the unifying capacity of the archetypal
hypothesis should not be misunderstood as a form of ultimate reductionism.
The nature of archetypes may be expressed through many distinct represen-
tations, each of which possesses a formal equivalency despite manifest dif-
ferences in content. The archetypal hypothesis is not a grand reductionist
scheme to reduce the social sciences to biology, biology to physics, and physics
to a Platonic version of mathematics. Rather, it indicates the possibility of the
emergence of a neutral language which might provide a basis for a common,
unifying understanding in many diverse areas of human thought. In this
